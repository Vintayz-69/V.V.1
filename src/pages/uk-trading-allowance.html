What is the trading allowance?

The trading allowance lets you earn up to £1,000 a year from self-employment, casual jobs (such as babysitting or gardening) or hiring out your own equipment without paying tax on it. GOV.UK calls it a tax exemption. It's on your gross income: the total before any expenses.

  • £1,000 or less of gross trading income in a tax year: you usually don't need to tell HMRC or pay tax on it.
  • More than £1,000: you must register for Self Assessment by 5 October after the tax year ends. On your return you can either take £1,000 off your income (the allowance) or take off your actual expenses. You can't do both.

There's a separate £1,000 property allowance for rental income. If you have both kinds of income, you get £1,000 for each.

How to use this calculator

  1. Enter your side income for the tax year, before taking off any costs.
  2. Enter the expenses you could claim if you didn't use the allowance.
  3. Enter your other taxable income, such as a salary, so the side income is taxed at the right rate.
  4. Choose where you live, because Scotland has its own Income Tax rates.

How it's calculated

Profit with the allowance = income − £1,000 (or − income, if under £1,000) Profit with your expenses = income − expenses Extra tax = Income Tax on (other income + profit) − Income Tax on other income alone + Class 4 National Insurance on the profit The lower profit leaves less tax. As a rule, the allowance wins when your expenses are under £1,000, and your expenses win when they're over £1,000.

Worked examples

All use 2026 to 2027 rates for England, Wales and Northern Ireland.

A small side business with few costs. Jamie earns a £30,000 salary and makes £3,000 selling crafts, with £400 of costs. With the allowance, the taxable profit is £2,000, and at 20% that's £400 of tax. With expenses, it's £2,600 and £520. The allowance leaves £120 less tax.

Higher costs. Same salary and income, but £1,800 of costs. Expenses leave £1,200 of profit and £240 of tax. The allowance leaves £2,000 and £400. Here expenses leave £160 less tax.

Under £1,000. £800 of side income with £100 of costs. The allowance covers all of it, so there's no tax to pay and usually no need to tell HMRC. Claiming the £100 of costs instead would leave £700 of profit and £140 of tax.

A higher-rate taxpayer. A £60,000 salary, £5,000 of side income and £500 of costs. Each pound of profit is taxed at 40%. The allowance leaves £4,000 and £1,600 of tax; expenses leave £4,500 and £1,800. The allowance leaves £200 less.

No other income. £10,000 of income and £2,000 of costs, with no salary. Either way the profit (£9,000 or £8,000) is under the £12,570 Personal Allowance, so there's no tax either way. You'd still need to register for Self Assessment, because your gross income is over £1,000.

Full-time self-employed. £20,000 of income, £500 of costs and no other income. The allowance leaves £19,000 of profit: £1,286 of Income Tax and £385.80 of Class 4 NI, £1,671.80 in total. Expenses leave £19,500 and £1,801.80. The allowance leaves £130 less.

When you can't use the allowance

GOV.UK says you can't use the trading or property allowance in a tax year if you have any trade or property income from:

  • your employer, or your spouse's or civil partner's employer
  • a company that you, or someone connected to you, owns or controls
  • a partnership where you, or someone connected to you, is a partner

The trading allowance doesn't apply to your share of income from a partnership.

When claiming expenses can matter more

  • If you made a loss. If your costs were more than your income, using your expenses shows a loss, which you may be able to use against other income. With the allowance, your profit is simply zero. This calculator shows a loss as no tax, and doesn't work out loss relief.
  • If your costs are over £1,000. Your actual expenses will usually leave a lower profit than the allowance.

Keep records of your income and costs either way. You need the income figure to know whether you're over £1,000, and HMRC can ask to see your records.

What this calculator doesn't cover

  • Other income types, such as dividends, savings interest or rental income. It treats your other income as salary or similar.
  • Pension contributions, student loans and Marriage Allowance.
  • Loss relief, as above.
  • Benefits and tax credits, which can count your income differently. GOV.UK has a section on this.
  • National Insurance on your salary. That doesn't change with your side income, so it isn't shown.

Frequently asked questions

Is the £1,000 trading allowance based on income or profit?

Income. GOV.UK says gross income is the total you'd put on your tax return before any allowances or expenses are taken off. £1,200 of sales with £300 of costs is £1,200 of income, which is over £1,000.

Can I claim the trading allowance and my expenses?

No. You choose one. GOV.UK says you can't deduct any other expenses or allowances if you claim the allowances.

I earned £1,500. Is only £500 taxed?

If you use the allowance, yes: £1,500 minus £1,000 leaves £500 of taxable profit. You'll need to register for Self Assessment and put it on your tax return.

I have a side business and rent out a room. Do I get £2,000?

You get £1,000 for trading income and a separate £1,000 for property income. Renting a room in your own home may come under the separate Rent a Room Scheme instead, with a £7,500 limit; our Rent a Room calculator works it out.
