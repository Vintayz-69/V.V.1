Why an S corporation can save tax

As a sole proprietor (or a single-member LLC taxed as one), you pay self-employment tax of 15.3% on 92.35% of your whole profit, up to the Social Security wage base. With an S corporation, you pay yourself a salary, and Social Security and Medicare are paid on that salary through payroll. The rest of the profit passes to you as S corporation income, without self-employment tax.

But there are costs that simple comparisons often leave out:

  • The QBI deduction gets smaller. Your salary isn't qualified business income, so the 20% deduction only applies to the profit after your salary. That means more income tax.
  • Running costs. Payroll, a separate business tax return, state fees and unemployment taxes on your salary.
  • Your salary must be reasonable. You can't pay yourself a tiny salary to avoid payroll taxes.

This calculator includes the first two, so the result is closer to what you'd really keep.

How it's calculated

Sole proprietor Self-employment tax = 15.3% × 92.35% of profit (12.4% part only up to the wage base) Income tax on: profit − ½ SE tax − standard deduction − QBI deduction QBI deduction = 20% × (profit − ½ SE tax), capped at 20% of taxable income S corporation Payroll taxes = 7.65% employer + 7.65% employee on your salary S corp income (K-1) = profit − costs − salary − employer's 7.65% Income tax on: salary + K-1 − standard deduction − QBI deduction QBI deduction = 20% × K-1 income only, capped at 20% of taxable income You keep = profit − costs − all of the taxes above

Worked examples

All single filers, 2026 federal figures, standard deduction and no other income.

$100,000 of profit, a $50,000 salary and $2,000 of costs. As a sole proprietor: $14,129.55 of self-employment tax and $8,235 of income tax (after a $15,367.05 QBI deduction), so you keep $77,635.45. As an S corporation: $7,650 of payroll taxes, K-1 income of $44,175, a smaller QBI deduction of $8,835 and $9,944.80 of income tax, so you keep $80,405.20. The S corporation leaves $2,769.75 more.

$50,000 of profit, a $30,000 salary, $2,000 of costs. You keep $40,267.93 as a sole proprietor and $40,482.32 as an S corporation: only $214.39 more.

$40,000 of profit, a $25,000 salary, $2,000 of costs. You keep $32,573.07 as a sole proprietor and $32,290.60 as an S corporation, $282.47 less. At this level the costs outweigh the saving.

$150,000 of profit, a $70,000 salary, $2,500 of costs. You keep $112,392.38 as a sole proprietor and $117,404.16 as an S corporation: $5,011.78 more.

A salary that's too high. $100,000 of profit with a $90,000 salary: payroll taxes of $13,770 and almost no QBI deduction. You'd keep $73,063.76, which is $4,571.69 less than as a sole proprietor.

What "reasonable compensation" means

The IRS says an S corporation must pay reasonable compensation to a shareholder-employee for their services before making non-wage distributions, and it can reclassify distributions as wages. The IRS lists factors such as:

  • your training and experience, duties and responsibilities
  • the time and effort you put into the business
  • what comparable businesses pay for similar services
  • what you pay employees who aren't shareholders

This calculator can't tell you what a reasonable salary is for your work. A tax professional can help you set one you can support.

How a business becomes an S corporation

The IRS says the corporation must file Form 2553, signed by all the shareholders. To qualify, it must be a domestic corporation with no more than 100 shareholders, only allowable shareholders (such as individuals and certain trusts and estates) and one class of stock. An S corporation passes its income, losses, deductions and credits through to its shareholders, who report them on their personal tax returns.

What this calculator doesn't cover

  • State taxes, including state income tax, franchise taxes and state rules on S corporations.
  • Unemployment taxes on your salary. Add an estimate to the costs.
  • Income over the QBI threshold ($201,750 of taxable income for single filers in 2026), where the deduction depends on wages and the type of business.
  • Retirement plan contributions and health insurance. The IRS has a separate page on S corporation compensation and medical insurance.
  • Other income, itemized deductions and credits.

Frequently asked questions

At what profit does an S corporation start saving tax?

It depends on your salary, costs and filing status. In our examples, $50,000 of profit saves very little and $40,000 costs more. Try your own numbers above.

Can I pay myself a very low salary?

No. The IRS says S corporation shareholder-employees must be paid reasonable compensation before distributions, and it can reclassify distributions as wages subject to employment taxes.

Why does my income tax go up as an S corporation?

Your salary doesn't count as qualified business income, so your QBI deduction is smaller. The calculator shows both deductions.

Is an LLC the same as an S corporation?

No. The IRS says an LLC with only one member is, by default, disregarded as separate from its owner for income tax, so it's taxed like a sole proprietorship unless it elects otherwise. The comparison here is about how the business is taxed.
