How compound interest works

With compound interest, the interest you earn is added to your balance. Next time, interest is worked out on that bigger balance, so you earn interest on your interest. In the first few years the difference is small. Over 10 or 20 years it becomes a large part of the total, which is why starting early matters more than the exact amount you start with.

Future Value of Principal: FV_p = P × (1 + r / n)^(n × t) Future Value of Deposits: FV_d = PMT × [ (1 + r / n)^(n × t) - 1 ] ÷ (r / n) Total Portfolio Balance: Total = FV_p + FV_d Where: P = Initial principal deposit PMT = Monthly deposit amount (added at the end of each month) r = Annual interest rate (decimal) n = Compounding periods per year (12 for monthly) t = Time in years

Worked examples

Saving every month. Sam starts with $5,000 and adds $200 at the end of every month for 5 years. The account pays 6% a year, compounded monthly.

Money put in = 5,000 + 200 × 60 months = $17,000.00 Ending balance = $20,698.26 Interest earned = 20,698.26 − 17,000 = $3,698.26

Leaving a lump sum alone. $10,000 left for 10 years at 7% a year, compounded monthly, with nothing added, grows to $20,096.61. The money roughly doubles, and more than half of the final balance is interest.

What this calculator doesn't include

  • Changing rates. It uses the same rate for every year. Savings rates go up and down, and investment returns aren't fixed at all: they can be negative in some years.
  • Inflation. The ending balance is in future money. Prices will probably be higher by then, so it will buy less than the same amount buys today.
  • Tax and fees. Interest and investment growth may be taxed, and accounts or funds may charge fees. Both lower the real result.

Use the result to compare options and see the effect of saving more or for longer, not as a promise of what you'll have.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is only paid on the money you put in. Compound interest is also paid on the interest you've already earned, so the balance grows faster each year.

What rate should I enter?

For a savings account, use the rate your bank shows. Banks in the UK usually quote it as AER and banks in the US as APY. Both are yearly rates that already include the effect of compounding, so choose "Annually" in the compounding box to avoid counting it twice. Investments don't have a set rate, so try a few different rates to see a range of results rather than relying on one number.

How does compounding frequency change the result?

The more often interest is added, the sooner it starts earning interest itself, so monthly compounding gives a slightly higher result than yearly compounding at the same rate. The difference is usually small compared with the effect of the rate and the number of years.

Is there a quick way to estimate how long money takes to double?

Yes, the "rule of 72": divide 72 by the yearly rate. At 7% that's about 10 years, which matches the lump sum example above. It's a rough guide that works best for rates between about 4% and 12%.
