How to use this calculator

  1. Add up your fixed costs for a period, such as a month. These are costs you pay even if you sell nothing: rent, insurance, software subscriptions, website hosting, and any regular pay you take for yourself.
  2. Enter your price per unit. A unit is whatever you sell: a product, a project, a session or an hour of work.
  3. Enter your variable cost per unit. These costs only happen when you make a sale: materials, packaging, postage, card fees and platform fees.

How it's calculated

Contribution Margin (CM) = Unit Selling Price − Unit Variable Cost Contribution Margin Ratio = (Contribution Margin ÷ Unit Selling Price) × 100 Break-Even Volume (Units) = Total Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution Margin Break-Even Revenue = Break-Even Volume × Unit Selling Price

The contribution margin is the part of each sale that is left to pay your fixed costs. Once those are paid, it becomes profit.

Worked examples

A small coffee roaster. Fixed costs are $5,000 a month. Each bag sells for $50 and costs $20 to make and send.

Contribution margin = 50 − 20 = $30 a bag (60%) Break-even volume = 5,000 ÷ 30 = 166.67 bags Break-even revenue = 5,000 ÷ 0.60 = $8,333.33

You can't sell two-thirds of a bag, so the roaster needs to sell 167 bags a month to cover its costs. Each bag after that adds $30 of profit.

A freelancer selling a digital product. A developer sells a software licence for $100. Payment fees and support cost about $40 per sale, and fixed costs are $12,000 a year.

Contribution margin = 100 − 40 = $60 a sale (60%) Break-even volume = 12,000 ÷ 60 = 200 licences a year Break-even revenue = 200 × 100 = $20,000

Using break-even if you sell your time

If you're a freelancer, a "unit" can be a billable hour or a typical project. Put your monthly business costs in fixed costs, your rate as the price, and any cost that comes with each job (such as platform fees) as the variable cost. The result is how many hours or projects you need each month before you make anything at all.

Remember that you can't bill every hour you work. If the break-even number is close to the most hours you could realistically bill, your rate is probably too low. What break-even doesn't tell you

  • Whether you can sell that much. The number is a target, not a forecast. Compare it with what you've actually sold before.
  • Cash timing. You can be above break-even and still run short of cash if customers pay late. Our late payment calculator helps when invoices are overdue.
  • Tax. The profit shown is before tax.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between fixed and variable costs?

Fixed costs stay the same whether you sell nothing or a thousand units, such as rent, insurance and software. Variable costs go up with each sale, such as materials, packaging, postage and payment fees.

What happens if my variable cost is higher than my price?

You lose money on every sale, so selling more makes the loss bigger. You can never break even unless you raise the price or lower the cost of each unit.

How can I lower my break-even point?

There are three ways: cut fixed costs, lower the cost of each unit, or raise your price. A higher price usually has the biggest effect, because all of the increase goes into the contribution margin.
