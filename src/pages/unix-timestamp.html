How to use this converter

  1. Paste a timestamp in the first box, or press "Now" for the current one. You'll see the date in UTC and in your own time zone.
  2. Leave the unit on "Detect" unless you know it. Timestamps with 12 or more digits are read as milliseconds, as JavaScript and many APIs use.
  3. To go the other way, pick a date and time, say which time zone it's in, and copy the timestamp.

What is a Unix timestamp?

A Unix timestamp (also called epoch time or POSIX time) counts the seconds since 00:00:00 UTC on 1 January 1970, not counting leap seconds. It's the same number everywhere in the world at the same moment, which is why computers use it to store times. Negative numbers are dates before 1970.

Date (UTC) = 1 January 1970 00:00:00 + timestamp seconds Milliseconds = seconds × 1,000

Worked examples

0 → Thursday 1 January 1970, 00:00:00 UTC 1700000000 → Tuesday 14 November 2023, 22:13:20 UTC 1700000000000 → the same moment, written in milliseconds 2147483647 → Tuesday 19 January 2038, 03:14:07 UTC 5 October 2026, 12:00:00 UTC → 1791201600

Frequently asked questions

What happens in 2038?

Older systems store timestamps as a signed 32-bit number, whose largest value is 2,147,483,647: 03:14:07 UTC on 19 January 2038. One second later those systems overflow. Modern systems use 64-bit numbers and are not affected.

Why is my converted time an hour out?

Usually the time zone. A timestamp is always UTC; your local time depends on your device's time zone and daylight saving time. When converting a date to a timestamp, make sure you've chosen the time zone the date was written in.

Does the timestamp include leap seconds?

No. Unix time treats every day as exactly 86,400 seconds, so leap seconds are not counted.
