How to use this calculator

  1. Enter the W-2 salary and what the benefits are worth to you each year.
  2. Enter the 1099 rate you've been offered.
  3. Be realistic about 1099 hours and weeks. Contractors aren't paid for vacation, holidays or sick days.

How it's calculated

Employer payroll tax = 6.2% of salary (up to $184,500) + 1.45% of salary W-2 package = salary + benefits + employer payroll tax 1099 paid hours = hours per week × weeks per year Break-even rate = W-2 package ÷ 1099 paid hours Difference = (1099 rate × paid hours) − W-2 package

We add the employer's payroll tax because, as a 1099 contractor, you pay both halves through self-employment tax. Income tax is roughly similar either way, so it's left out of the comparison.

Worked example

Riley is offered a W-2 job at $90,000 with benefits worth $12,000, or a 1099 contract at $65 an hour for about 40 hours a week, 48 weeks a year.

Employer payroll tax = 90,000 × 7.65% = 6,885 W-2 package = 90,000 + 12,000 + 6,885 = 108,885 1099 paid hours = 40 × 48 = 1,920 Break-even rate = 108,885 ÷ 1,920 = $56.71/hour 1099 income = 65 × 1,920 = 124,800 Difference = 124,800 − 108,885 = +$15,915

The 1099 contract needs at least $56.71 an hour to match the W-2 package. At $65 it's worth about $15,915 more a year, before business costs such as insurance, equipment and accounting.

Second example: when the W-2 job wins

Taylor, a bookkeeper, has two offers. Offer A is a W-2 job at $75,000 a year with health insurance and a retirement match that Taylor values at $9,000. Offer B is a 1099 contract at $50 an hour. The $50 sounds high next to the W-2 job's $36.06 an hour ($75,000 ÷ 2,080), but the contract is for about 35 hours a week, and Taylor plans 6 weeks a year without work for vacation, holidays and sick days.

  1. Value the W-2 offer fully. Employer payroll tax = 75,000 × 7.65% = $5,737.50. W-2 package = 75,000 + 9,000 + 5,737.50 = $89,737.50.
  2. Count the hours the contract actually pays. 35 hours × 46 weeks = 1,610 hours.
  3. Find the break-even rate. 89,737.50 ÷ 1,610 = $55.74 an hour.
  4. Compare. The contract pays 50 × 1,610 = $80,500, which is $9,237.50 less than the W-2 package, even before Taylor pays for business costs.

To match Offer A, Taylor would need to ask for about $56 an hour, or more billable hours. This is why the hourly rate alone can mislead: the hours you can bill and the benefits you give up matter as much.

What this calculator doesn't cover

  • Business expenses you'd pay as a contractor, and the tax deductions they bring.
  • Job security, unemployment insurance and workers' compensation, which W-2 employees usually have.
  • Differences in income tax from the QBI deduction or retirement plans.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between 1099 and W-2?

W-2 workers are employees: the employer withholds tax, pays half the payroll tax and often provides benefits. 1099 workers are independent contractors who pay their own taxes, including both halves of Social Security and Medicare.

Is there a quick rule of thumb?

There's no official one, and the two examples on this page show why: Riley's contract needed $56.71 an hour against W-2 pay of $43.27 an hour (31% more), while Taylor's needed $55.74 against $36.06 (55% more). The answer depends on the benefits you'd give up and the hours you can really bill, so it's worth working out each time.

What benefits should I count?

Count what you'd otherwise pay for yourself: the employer's share of health insurance, retirement matching, paid vacation and holidays (already reflected if you lower your 1099 weeks), and anything else with a cash value to you, such as a phone or training budget.

Do 1099 contractors pay more tax?

They pay self-employment tax, which includes both halves of Social Security and Medicare, and they handle their own tax through quarterly payments. Half of the self-employment tax is deductible, and business expenses lower taxable profit. Our self-employment tax calculator and quarterly estimated tax calculator show the amounts.

Can an employer choose whether I'm 1099 or W-2?

Not freely. Worker classification depends on how much control the company has over your work. If you're unsure, see the IRS guidance on independent contractors versus employees.
