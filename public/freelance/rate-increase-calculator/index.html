Why a rate rise is less risky than it feels

The worry with raising your rates is losing clients. But at a higher rate, each hour you keep earns more, so you can lose some work and still earn the same. This calculator shows exactly how much. For example, a 20% rate rise means you could lose a sixth of your work (16.67%) and your income wouldn't change, while working fewer hours.

How to use this calculator

  1. Enter your current rate and your new rate. They can be hourly or daily, as long as both are the same kind.
  2. Enter how many hours (or days) you bill in a typical month at your current rate.
  3. Guess how much work you might lose when you raise your rate, such as a client who may leave. Try a few numbers to see the range.

How it's calculated

Rate increase = (new rate ÷ current rate − 1) × 100 Income now = current rate × hours a month Break-even loss = (1 − current rate ÷ new rate) × 100 Hours to earn same = income now ÷ new rate Income after loss = new rate × hours × (1 − work lost %)

The break-even loss is always smaller than the rate increase. A 20% rise lets you lose 16.67% of your work, not 20%, because the remaining work is a smaller base.

Worked examples

A 20% rise. Alex bills 100 hours a month at $75 and raises the rate to $90. Alex expects to lose about 10% of the work.

Rate increase = 90 ÷ 75 − 1 = 20.0% Income now = 75 × 100 = $7,500 Break-even loss = 1 − 75 ÷ 90 = 16.7% Hours to earn same = 7,500 ÷ 90 = 83.3 hours Income after loss = 90 × 100 × 0.90 = $8,100 (+$600)

Even after losing 10% of the work, Alex earns $600 more a month while working 10 fewer hours.

A small rise with a bigger loss. Jamie raises £50 to £55 (10%) on 80 hours a month, but expects to lose 15% of the work. The break-even loss is only 9.1%, so income falls from £4,000 to £3,740, a drop of £260 a month. With a small rise, keeping clients matters more.

Exactly break-even. A rise from $100 to $125 (25%) on 60 hours a month allows a 20% loss. Losing exactly 20% leaves 48 hours at $125, which is $6,000, the same as now.

Tips for raising your rates

  • Give notice. Tell existing clients well ahead, for example a month or two, so the change doesn't come as a surprise.
  • Start with new clients. Quoting your new rate to new clients first is a low-risk way to test it.
  • Explain the value, not your costs. Clients care about results: faster turnaround, experience, the work you've delivered for them.
  • Review your rates regularly, for example once a year, so each rise is small rather than one big jump after years at the same rate.

What this calculator doesn't include

  • Tax. All figures are before income tax.
  • New clients you might win at the new rate, which would add to the income shown.
  • Timing. It compares one typical month before and after. In real life, clients may move to the new rate at different times.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I raise my rates?

There's no single right amount. Start from the rate you need to reach your income goal, then look at what similar freelancers charge. This calculator then shows how much work you could afford to lose at that rate.

Can I use this for a day rate or a monthly retainer?

For a day rate, yes: enter days instead of hours. For retainers, enter the monthly fee as the rate and 1 as the "hours", and the work lost as the chance of losing that retainer. Our retainer calculator helps you set the fee itself.

What if I'm lowering my rate?

This calculator is for rate rises, so the new rate must be higher. To see what a lower rate earns at different hours, use the hourly to annual income calculator.
