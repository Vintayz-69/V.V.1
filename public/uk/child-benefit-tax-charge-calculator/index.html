How the High Income Child Benefit Charge works

If you or your partner get Child Benefit and either of you has adjusted net income over £60,000, the one with the higher income pays a tax charge. GOV.UK says you pay back 1% of your Child Benefit for every £200 over £60,000, so at £80,000 or more you pay back all of it. Below £60,000, there's no charge.

It's based on each partner's own income, not the household's. Two partners each earning £55,000 pay no charge; one earning £70,000 with a partner earning nothing pays it.

What adjusted net income is

Adjusted net income is your total taxable income (salary, profit from self-employment, savings interest, dividends, rent and so on) with some things taken off. GOV.UK's guide says to:

  • take off pension contributions paid before tax, such as through your employer's payroll
  • take off £1.25 for every £1 you paid into a personal pension that adds basic-rate tax relief for you
  • take off £1.25 for every £1 of Gift Aid donations
  • take off trading losses you're claiming relief for

Worked examples

All use 2026 to 2027 Child Benefit for 52 weeks.

GOV.UK's example (Clara). Taxable income of £70,000, less £4,750 of pension contributions paid before tax, is £65,250. Taking off £1,250 for £1,000 of Gift Aid leaves adjusted net income of £64,000. That's £4,000 over £60,000, or 20 steps of £200, so 20% of Child Benefit is paid back. With two children (£2,337.40), the charge is £467.48.

£70,000 with two children. £10,000 over is 50 steps, so half of £2,337.40: £1,168.70.

£70,000 with one child. Half of £1,406.60: £703.30.

A personal pension contribution. The same £70,000 earner with two children pays £4,000 into a personal pension with relief at source. Their adjusted net income falls by £5,000 to £65,000, so the charge is 25%: £584.35 instead of £1,168.70.

Over £80,000. £85,000 with three children: all £3,268.20 is paid back.

Child Benefit for part of the year. A baby born half-way through the year, with 26 weeks of Child Benefit for one child (£703.30) and £70,000 of income: half is paid back, £351.65.

Paying the charge, or opting out

  • Through PAYE or Self Assessment. GOV.UK says you can pay it through your tax code or on a Self Assessment tax return. If you already send a tax return, you pay it there. Self-employed people usually do.
  • Opting out. You can choose to stop getting Child Benefit payments and not pay the charge. GOV.UK says you still get National Insurance credits, which count towards your State Pension.

What this calculator doesn't cover

  • Part-steps of £200. It counts whole £200 steps (for example, £61,300 counts as 6 steps, 6%). We couldn't confirm on an official page how a part-step is treated, so for an exact figure use HMRC's own calculator.
  • Changes during the year, such as a new partner, or children joining or leaving your claim part-way through.
  • Working out your taxable income. Our take-home pay and self-employed tax calculators can help.
  • Tax years before 2026 to 2027. Child Benefit rates and, before 2024 to 2025, the thresholds were different.

Frequently asked questions

Is the £60,000 based on household income?

No. It's based on each person's own adjusted net income. The partner with the higher income pays the charge, if their income is over £60,000.

At what income do I pay back all my Child Benefit?

At £80,000 of adjusted net income or more. Between £60,000 and £80,000 you pay back part of it.

Do pension contributions reduce the charge?

They reduce your adjusted net income, which the charge is based on. GOV.UK's guide shows how. Whether paying more into a pension suits you is a wider decision; a financial adviser or accountant can help.

Should we still claim Child Benefit if the charge takes all of it?

GOV.UK says that if you choose not to get the payments, you should still make a claim to get the other advantages. These include National Insurance credits, which count towards your State Pension; you get them automatically if your child is under 12.
